# Policy Instrument Diagnostic

An interactive teaching tool that illustrates how behavioral assumptions may influence policy design choices.

## What This Is

This applet accompanies Chapter 6 in the **Policy Design and Governance** book by Cali Curley, Saba Siddiki, and Tomás Olivier. It puts students in the role of a policy designer and asks them to do two things:

1. **Describe a target population** by adjusting five behavioral dimensions: awareness, capacity to comply, motivation, trust in institutions, and social norm alignment.
2. **Choose a policy instrument** — such as an information campaign, financial incentive, regulation, nudge, facilitation program, or community-based approach.

The tool then reveals the **assumptions embedded in the chosen instrument** and diagnoses whether those assumptions match the population profile. When they don't, it explains why the mismatch matters and how it would affect policy uptake.

A fit ranking compares all six instruments against the current profile, reinforcing the core insight: no instrument is universally "best", each one is a bet on a particular theory of why people aren't complying.

## How to Use It

Visit the live version at: `https://tomaso9.github.io/PDandC_Chapter_6/`

No installation or login required. Works in any modern browser on desktop, tablet, or mobile.

### Preset Scenarios

The applet includes four preset population profiles to get started quickly:

- **Well-resourced, disengaged** — people who can comply but don't want to
- **Willing but constrained** — people who want to comply but face practical barriers
- **Low-trust community** — moderate capacity and motivation, but deep institutional distrust
- **Uninformed population** — low awareness despite adequate capacity and motivation

These are starting points. Students are encouraged to adjust the sliders and explore what shifts.

## Suggested Exercises

- **Mismatch hunt:** Set a population profile, choose the *worst-fitting* instrument, and articulate why it fails. Then find the best fit and explain what makes it work.
- **Same population, different instruments:** Pick a preset and cycle through all six instruments. Which ones flag mismatches? Which don't? What does that tell you about what each instrument takes for granted?
- **Reverse engineering:** Start from a real-world policy you know. Based on the instrument it uses, what must the designers have assumed about the target population? Reconstruct the implicit behavioral profile.
- **Designer bias:** Have students each independently choose an instrument for the same population profile, then compare. Do they converge? What drives the disagreements?

## Technical Details

The applet is a single self-contained HTML file (`index.html`) with no server dependencies. No data is collected or transmitted.
